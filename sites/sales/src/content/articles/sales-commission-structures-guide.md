A sales commission structure is the framework that defines how sales representatives earn variable compensation based on their performance. The right structure aligns rep behavior with company revenue goals, motivates consistent quota attainment, and keeps top performers from leaving for better comp plans. According to [Salesforce State of Sales](https://www.salesforce.com/stateofsales/), compensation misalignment is one of the top three reasons high-performing reps leave their roles, making plan design a strategic priority, not just an HR exercise.

This guide compares the seven most common commission structures, breaks down how to choose the right one for your team size and sales cycle, and shows real examples by industry. Whether you are building a comp plan for a five-person SDR team or restructuring an enterprise sales org, you will find actionable frameworks here.

## What Are the Main Sales Commission Structures?

There are seven primary sales commission structures that B2B teams use, each with distinct trade-offs between income stability for reps and performance incentive for the company. The right choice depends on deal size, sales cycle length, and whether your team is hunting new logos or farming existing accounts. Most organizations blend two or three structures to balance base pay security with variable upside.

### Straight Commission

In a straight commission structure, reps earn no base salary and are paid purely on closed revenue. The commission rate is typically higher than other models, often 10-20% of closed-won deals, to compensate for the income risk. This structure is most common in real estate, insurance, and high-volume transactional sales where deals close quickly and reps control the full cycle.

The advantage is maximum upside for top performers and zero fixed cost for the employer. The risk is severe: according to HubSpot, pure-commission roles have 40% higher annual attrition than roles with a base salary. Reps face income volatility that makes it hard to sustain effort during dry spells, and the structure attracts hunters but repels farmers who drive retention and expansion revenue.

### Base Salary Plus Commission

This is the most common structure in B2B sales. Reps receive a fixed base salary plus a variable commission component tied to quota attainment. The typical split is 50/50, meaning base salary represents 50% of on-target earnings (OTE) and commission represents the other 50%. According to Salesforce, this split is standard for account executives at SaaS companies with deal sizes between $10,000 and $100,000.

The base salary provides income stability that improves retention and allows reps to invest time in relationship-building activities that do not pay off immediately. The commission component preserves performance incentive. The trade-off is higher fixed costs for the employer and less aggressive upside for top performers compared to straight commission.

### Tiered Commission

Tiered commission structures pay an increasing commission rate as reps hit higher revenue thresholds. A rep might earn 8% on the first $100,000 of closed revenue, 12% on the next $100,000, and 15% on everything above $200,000. According to Gartner sales compensation research, tiered plans drive 18-25% more revenue per rep than flat-rate plans because they reward reps for pushing past quota instead of coasting.

Tiered structures work best when you want to motivate reps to overachieve consistently. They are less effective for junior reps who may see the higher tiers as unreachable and give up early. Pair tiered rates with realistic quotas and clear visibility into progress so reps can see exactly how much more they earn by closing one more deal.

### Revenue vs. Margin-Based Commission

Revenue-based commission pays a percentage of total deal revenue, while margin-based commission pays a percentage of gross profit. Revenue-based plans are simpler to calculate and communicate, which makes them popular for early-stage teams. Margin-based plans are more complex but prevent reps from discounting heavily to win deals, since a discounted deal produces less commission.

Margin-based structures are essential when your product mix includes both high-margin and low-margin offerings. If a rep earns the same 10% on a high-margin SaaS subscription and a low-margin services engagement, they will prioritize whichever closes fastest, not whichever is most profitable. According to [Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2023/01/how-to-design-a-sales-compensation-plan), margin-based plans are one of the strongest tools for aligning rep behavior with company profitability.

### Salary Plus Bonus

In this structure, reps receive a full salary with a discretionary or formula-based bonus tied to team or company performance rather than individual quota. This is common in customer success and account management roles where retention and satisfaction matter more than new revenue. The bonus might be 15-30% of base salary, paid quarterly or annually.

This structure prioritizes collaboration over individual competition, which suits teams where multiple people contribute to each deal. The downside is weaker individual incentive; reps who close the most deals earn the same bonus as those who close the least, which can frustrate top performers and drive them to commission-heavy roles elsewhere.

### Residual Commission

Residual commission pays reps a smaller ongoing percentage of revenue from accounts they closed, for as long as the account remains a customer. This structure is standard in SaaS and subscription businesses, where a rep might earn 5% of first-year revenue and 2% of renewal revenue annually. It aligns rep incentives with long-term retention and discourages aggressive closing tactics that lead to early churn.

Residual structures create compound earnings for tenured reps, which improves retention. They also increase comp costs over time as the book of residual-paying accounts grows. Most SaaS companies cap residual payments at 2-3 years or reduce the rate each year to manage this cost trajectory.

### SPIFs and Variable Incentives

A SPIF (Sales Performance Incentive Fund) is a short-term bonus layered on top of the regular commission plan to drive specific behaviors. Common SPIFs include $500 for each demo booked during a product launch, $1,000 for closing a deal with a specific competitor displacement, or a team-wide bonus for hitting a quarterly target. SPIFs typically run for 30-90 days and are paid separately from regular commission.

SPIFs are powerful for redirecting rep attention quickly without restructuring the entire comp plan. According to [LinkedIn Sales Solutions](https://business.linkedin.com/sales-solutions/b2b-sales-strategy-guide), well-designed SPIFs can increase focus on a target product by 40% during the incentive period. The risk is SPIF fatigue: if you run them constantly, reps start to expect them and game the system rather than selling organically.

> **Pro tip:** Run no more than two SPIFs per quarter. Constant short-term incentives train reps to wait for the next bonus before pushing hard, which erodes the baseline commission plan's effectiveness.

## How to Choose the Right Commission Structure for Your Team

Choosing a commission structure requires evaluating your average deal size, sales cycle length, team maturity, and revenue model. There is no universal best structure; the right plan is the one that aligns rep incentives with your specific business economics. According to Gartner, companies that match their commission structure to their sales motion see 30% fewer instances of behavior misaligned with company goals than those that copy competitor plans blindly.

### Match Structure to Sales Cycle Length

Short sales cycles (under 30 days) support higher variable components because reps see payoff quickly and can sustain motivation through rapid wins. Inside sales teams selling transactional products often use a 40/60 base-to-variable split or even straight commission. The fast feedback loop means reps can adjust behavior weekly based on results.

Long sales cycles (90+ days) require larger base salaries. Enterprise reps working six-month deals cannot sustain themselves on commission alone, and the delayed payoff erodes motivation if base pay is too low. A 60/40 or 70/30 base-to-variable split is standard for enterprise teams. According to Salesforce, enterprise account executives average $180,000 OTE with a 60/40 split, reflecting the longer ramp and higher skill requirements of complex deal cycles.

For teams building an [inside sales function](/b2b-sales/b2b-inside-sales-guide) with a mix of cycle lengths, consider different comp plans for different segments rather than forcing one structure across all reps.

### Align with Deal Size and Revenue Model

Deal size directly affects commission rate design. Small deals ($1,000-$10,000) need higher percentage rates (10-15%) to make each close worthwhile for the rep. Large deals ($100,000+) work with lower rates (3-5%) because the dollar value of each close is substantial. A rep closing a $250,000 enterprise deal at 4% earns $10,000, which is competitive with closing twenty-five $10,000 deals at 10%.

For subscription businesses, residual commission aligns rep behavior with retention. If you pay commission only on first-year ARR, reps have no incentive to ensure the customer renews. According to HubSpot, SaaS companies that pay residual commission on renewals see 15% lower gross churn than those that pay first-year-only commission. This aligns with broader thinking about [customer acquisition cost](https://marketing.growthgear.com.au/seo/customer-acquisition-cost-calculation-optimization-guide), where retaining existing accounts is far cheaper than winning new ones.

### Consider Team Maturity and Role

Early-stage startups with founders selling need simpler plans than mature sales orgs with specialized roles. A five-person team can run on a single base-plus-commission structure. A fifty-person org with SDRs, AEs, and customer success managers needs role-specific plans: SDRs compensated on meetings booked and SQLs generated, AEs on closed-won revenue, and CSMs on net revenue retention.

This is especially important when [structuring your sales team](/b2b-sales/b2b-sales-team-structure-guide), as each role's comp plan must reinforce its specific function rather than creating overlap or conflict between roles.

## Commission Structure Examples by Industry and Deal Size

Commission structures vary significantly by industry because deal economics, sales cycles, and product margins differ widely. A plan that works for a high-volume SaaS inside sales team will fail in a low-volume enterprise consulting business. Here are concrete examples of how companies structure compensation across different B2B contexts.

### SaaS: Base + Tiered Commission with Residual

A mid-market SaaS company selling $25,000 annual contracts typically uses a 50/50 base-to-variable split with an OTE of $120,000. The rep earns a $60,000 base plus 10% commission on new ARR up to quota ($600,000 annually), with an accelerator to 15% above quota. Renewals pay 2% residual for two years. This structure balances new logo acquisition with retention incentive.

According to Salesforce, 65% of SaaS companies use some form of tiered or accelerated commission, making it the dominant model in the category. The accelerator above quota is critical because it costs the company less per incremental dollar of overachievement while giving reps strong reason to keep pushing past target.

### Professional Services: Margin-Based Commission

A consulting firm selling $200,000 engagements with 40% gross margins cannot afford to pay 10% of revenue as commission. Instead, they pay 15% of gross margin, which equals 6% of revenue. This structure protects profitability when reps negotiate discounts, because a discounted deal has a lower margin and therefore lower commission. It also incentivizes reps to sell higher-margin services.

Margin-based plans require accurate cost tracking and margin calculation for each deal, which demands a CRM that captures cost data alongside revenue. Teams that lack this infrastructure should start with revenue-based plans and migrate to margin-based as their data maturity improves. Leveraging [AI tools for data analysis](https://ai.growthgear.com.au/ai-tools/best-ai-tools-for-data-analysis) can help automate margin tracking and flag low-margin deals before they are signed.

### Enterprise Hardware: Long-Cycle Base + Commission with Milestone Payments

Enterprise hardware deals often take 6-12 months to close and involve procurement, legal, and technical evaluation. Reps need a higher base salary (70% of OTE) to sustain effort through the long cycle. Commission is paid at 3-5% of deal value, but to manage cash flow, some companies split commission into milestone payments: 30% at signed PO, 40% at delivery, and 30% at acceptance.

This structure mirrors the [territory planning approach](/b2b-sales/sales-territory-planning-guide) used in enterprise sales, where long cycles require sustained multi-quarter effort and reps need compensation that does not force them to rush deals to make rent.

## Common Mistakes That Break Commission Plans

Most commission plan failures share the same root causes: misaligned incentives, unclear rules, and plans designed in isolation from the sales strategy they are meant to support. According to Gartner, 60% of sales organizations revise their comp plan annually, and the majority of revisions fix problems that better upfront design would have prevented. Here are the most common mistakes and how to avoid them.

### Capping Commission Earnings

A commission cap limits the total variable pay a rep can earn in a period. Companies impose caps to control costs, but the unintended consequence is that top performers stop selling once they hit the cap. According to HubSpot, capped plans are the number one reason top quartile reps leave for uncapped competitors. If a rep can earn more by selling less at another company, they will.

Instead of capping, use decelerators: reduce the commission rate above a certain threshold rather than cutting it to zero. A rep earning 15% up to $300,000 and 8% above that still has incentive to keep selling, just at a lower marginal rate. This controls costs while preserving the behavior you want.

### Paying the Same Rate on All Products

Flat commission rates across all products create a hidden problem: reps will prioritize the easiest-to-close product, not the most strategic one. If your company sells a high-margin flagship product and a low-margin add-on, paying 10% on both means reps will push the add-on because it closes faster, even though it generates less profit.

The fix is product-tiered commission rates. Pay 12% on the flagship product, 8% on the add-on, and 5% on services. This steers rep effort toward the products that matter most to company profitability. According to HBR, product-differentiated commission rates are one of the most underused but highest-impact levers in comp plan design.

### Setting Quotas Without Historical Data

Quotas set by intuition or top-down revenue targets rather than historical performance data create immediate distrust. Reps who believe their quota is unachievable will disengage before the quarter even starts. According to Salesforce, quota attainment rates below 60% correlate strongly with rep attrition and lowered morale across the entire team.

Use a data-driven quota-setting process: analyze historical close rates by territory, account for market growth, and set quotas that 60-70% of reps can achieve. Top performers should overachieve by 120-150%, creating a realistic distribution that motivates the middle of the pack rather than demoralizing them. This connects to broader [salesperson strategies for hitting quota](/sales-techniques/salesperson-strategies-hit-quota-consistently) consistently.

### Ignoring Plan Complexity

A comp plan with twelve components, five accelerators, and three SPIFs running simultaneously is impossible for reps to calculate in their heads. When reps cannot predict their earnings from a given deal, they lose motivation. According to Gartner, reps on plans they describe as "too complex to understand" sell 12% less than reps on plans they can calculate manually.

> **Common mistake:** Adding a new accelerator or modifier every time a problem surfaces. After three quarters of patches, the plan is so complex that no one, including sales ops, can accurately forecast comp costs. Start simple and redesign holistically rather than patching incrementally.

### Failing to Align with Sales Strategy

Your commission plan is a direct expression of your sales strategy. If your strategy prioritizes new logo acquisition but your comp plan pays the same rate on renewals and upsells, reps will farm existing accounts because it is easier. Every comp plan decision should trace back to a specific strategic goal, whether that is market penetration, product mix, or customer retention. This is why [b2b sales strategy](/b2b-sales/b2b-sales-strategy-guide) and compensation design must be developed together, not sequentially.

## How to Design a Commission Plan That Scales

A scalable commission plan works for a five-rep team and a fifty-rep team without fundamental redesign. Scalability comes from simplicity, clear documentation, and alignment with your CRM and billing systems. According to Gartner, companies that design for scalability from the start spend 40% less time on comp plan administration than those that redesign every time they double headcount.

### Start with OTE and Work Backward

Define the target OTE for each role based on market data, then work backward to determine the base-to-variable split and commission rate. If the market OTE for a mid-market AE is $130,000 and you want a 50/50 split, the base is $65,000 and the variable target is $65,000. If the average rep closes $650,000 in revenue annually, the commission rate is 10% ($65,000 / $650,000). This approach ensures your comp costs are sustainable at scale.

### Build in Accelerators and Decelerators

A scalable plan needs built-in mechanisms for overachievement and underperformance. Accelerators above quota (15% instead of 10%) reward top performers without requiring a plan redesign. Decelerators below quota (5% instead of 10%) protect company margins when reps underperform. These mechanisms handle the full performance distribution without ad hoc adjustments.

### Document Everything and Automate Calculation

Write a comp plan document that any rep can read and understand in ten minutes. Include the base salary, commission rate, quota, accelerator thresholds, and payment schedule. Then automate calculation through your CRM so reps can see their current earnings in real time. Manual spreadsheet calculations do not scale past ten reps and create disputes that erode trust. Connecting compensation to [CRM-driven sales funnels](https://marketing.growthgear.com.au/content-marketing/how-to-create-high-converting-sales-funnels-complete-guide) ensures that every commission calculation traces back to a verified pipeline stage.

### Test the Plan Before Launch

Model the plan against the previous year's actual results before launching. Run every rep's closed deals through the new formula and compare projected earnings to actual earnings. If the new plan would have cut earnings for 30% of reps, you have an adoption problem before you start. Adjust the plan until the distribution of winners and losers matches your strategic intent.

### Review Quarterly, Redesign Annually

Quarterly reviews catch problems early: Is quota attainment clustering at 40%? Are reps avoiding a specific product? Is one territory overperforming by 200%? Annual redesigns address structural issues that quarterly tweaks cannot fix. According to Gartner, plans reviewed quarterly and redesigned annually have 30% fewer behavior misalignment issues than plans that are set and forgotten.
