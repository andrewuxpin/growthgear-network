A mutual action plan (MAP) is one of the highest-leverage tools a B2B sales team can deploy in the back half of a deal. It converts a fuzzy "we're working on it" into a shared, dated checklist that both seller and buyer own. This guide covers what a MAP is, when to introduce it, how to build one with your buyer, the mistakes that stall deals, and how to maintain it through signature.

## What Is a Mutual Action Plan?

A mutual action plan is a shared, co-created document between seller and buyer that lists every step both parties must complete from evaluation through contract signature, with named owners and target dates for each milestone. Unlike a seller-only close plan, a MAP is co-authored so both sides carry accountability for moving the deal forward on an agreed timeline.

The core insight is simple: deals stall when only one side feels urgency. A MAP distributes that urgency across both organisations by making the buying process visible and dated. When the buyer's procurement lead sees their own name next to a security review milestone, the milestone stops being "the vendor's problem" and starts being theirs.

### The Core Components of a MAP

Every MAP should contain five components:

- **Milestones**: every step from evaluation kickoff through signature, including the buyer's internal approvals.
- **Owners**: a named individual on each side responsible for completing each milestone, never a team or department.
- **Target dates**: a specific calendar date for each milestone, not "end of Q3."
- **Success criteria**: what "done" looks like for each step, such as "security review signed off with no open findings."
- **Dependencies**: which milestones block others, so slippage on one step visibly cascades.

### How a MAP Differs From a Close Plan or Joint Execution Plan

The terminology in this space is messy. Sales teams use "mutual close plan," "joint execution plan," and "mutual action plan" interchangeably, but there is a meaningful distinction in how each is used in practice. The table below maps the differences.

| Document | Who authors it | Whose steps it lists | Accountability model |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Seller close plan** | Seller alone | Seller's steps only (send proposal, follow up) | One-sided; buyer has no ownership |
| **Mutual action plan** | Seller + buyer together | Both parties' steps (procurement, security, legal, exec sign-off) | Shared; both sides own dates |

A seller-only close plan overlaps with the internal planning half of [Strategic Selling (Miller Heiman)](/sales-techniques/strategic-selling-miller-heiman-guide), whose Blue Sheet maps buying influences and red flags before the seller ever brings a MAP to the buyer. The two tools are complementary, not competing: the Blue Sheet is your internal map, and the MAP is the co-authored document you build once that map is solid enough to share.
| **MEDDPICC fields** | Seller (internal qualification) | Paper process, decision criteria | Seller's internal tracking, not shared with buyer |

A MAP is the only one of these that the buyer actually co-owns. That co-ownership is the entire mechanism. If you build a document listing your steps and email it to the buyer, you have a close plan, not a MAP, regardless of what you call it.

The success criteria written into a strong MAP usually trace back to the "Metrics That Matter" a rep uncovered during discovery, the same concept at the center of Force Management's [Command of the Message](/sales-techniques/command-of-the-message-methodology-guide) framework. A MAP without buyer-specific metrics tends to collapse into a generic project timeline instead of a document either side is motivated to keep updated.

## When to Introduce a Mutual Action Plan in Your Deal

Introduce the mutual action plan after discovery is complete and the buyer has confirmed the evaluation is moving forward, typically at the first substantive working session. Introducing it at proposal stage is too late because the buyer's internal approval chain is already running on its own undocumented timeline by then.

Timing is the most common point of failure for MAP adoption. Sellers who wait until the proposal is drafted are reacting to a buying process that has already started without them. The buyer's procurement team has likely already begun its own internal review calendar, and your MAP becomes a retroactive overlay rather than the operating document for the deal.

### The Discovery-to-MAP Handoff

The right moment to introduce the MAP is the meeting where you transition from diagnosing the buyer's problem to scoping the solution. By that point you should know the decision criteria, the economic buyer, and the rough timeline. The handoff sounds like this: "Based on what we've discussed, here's how I'd suggest we structure the rest of this evaluation so we hit your go-live date. Let's build this together."

That last sentence is doing the heavy lifting. You are not presenting a plan; you are opening a shared document and inviting the buyer to populate it with you. According to [Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/sales/insights), 77% of B2B buyers describe their own purchase as "very complex," which means most buyers are grateful for a structured plan when one is offered collaboratively.

### Why Introducing Too Late Kills the Deal

When the MAP arrives at proposal stage, the buyer's organisation has already started its own invisible process. Procurement may have a 21-day review window that started two weeks ago. Security review may be queued behind three other vendors. By the time you ask "what are the next steps on your side," the answers are already locked into someone else's calendar, and your target close date slips by a month.

[HubSpot sales data](https://www.hubspot.com/sales-statistics) shows that deals using a documented close plan close roughly 30% faster than those without one. The mechanism is not magic: the plan surfaces the buyer's internal steps early enough to sequence around them, rather than discovering them as blockers in week nine of the cycle.

## How to Build a Mutual Action Plan With Your Buyer

Build a mutual action plan by co-authoring it live with your champion in a shared document, listing every milestone the buyer's organisation needs to complete (procurement review, security review, legal redline, executive sign-off, signature) alongside your own, with named owners and dates agreed in the meeting rather than filled in afterward.

The build process is a working session, not an email exchange. Open a shared Google Doc or Notion page on screen during a call and populate it together. The act of the buyer typing their procurement lead's name and a date into the document is what converts a seller agenda into a shared plan.

### Step 1: Map the Buyer's Internal Buying Process

Before the build session, ask your champion to walk you through how purchases like this get approved inside their company. If you haven't already confirmed this is a real champion and not just a friendly contact, see [how to identify and test a B2B sales champion](/b2b-sales/how-to-build-a-b2b-sales-champion) first. You are looking for:

- The formal procurement or purchasing workflow (vendor onboarding, PO generation)
- The security or IT review process (questionnaire, third-party risk assessment)
- The legal review (MSA redline, order form)
- The economic buyer's sign-off (who signs, what they need to see)
- Any board, finance, or IT committee gates above a certain deal size

Capture these as a list of steps with the buyer's internal owners. This is the part most sellers skip, and it is the part that makes the MAP work. A deal is a sequence of approvals inside the buyer's company; your job is to make that sequence visible.

### Step 2: Co-Create Milestones and Dates Live

In the build session, put the shared document on screen and write milestones in real time. For each milestone, ask the buyer's champion: "Who owns this on your side, and what date can we target?" Write down whatever they say. If the answer is "I need to check with procurement," that becomes its own milestone with a date to confirm the owner.

The discipline here is to resist filling in dates yourself. A date the buyer commits to in the room is a commitment; a date you write on their behalf is a guess that they will quietly miss. Per [Salesforce State of Sales](https://www.salesforce.com/content/sales/en/blog/sales/buyer-aligned-selling), 75% of B2B buyers prefer a rep-guided digital journey, which means buyers want a structured process; they just do not want the seller dictating it.

### Step 3: Assign Owners on Both Sides

Every milestone gets a named owner. "Procurement team" is not an owner; "Priya in procurement" is. The same applies on your side: "send the security questionnaire response" should be owned by "Marcus in solutions engineering," not by "our team."

A MAP with unnamed owners is a wishlist. A MAP with named owners is a plan. When a milestone slips, you need to know exactly whose calendar to check, and "the procurement team" is too diffuse a target for that conversation to be productive.

### Step 4: Define Success Criteria and Exit Conditions

For each milestone, define what "done" looks like. "Security review complete" is vague; "security review signed off with no open findings, or with a remediation plan accepted by both parties" is a verifiable exit condition. Without this, milestones get marked complete when they are merely "in progress," and the deal drifts.

The build process below summarises the four steps and what each produces.

| Step | What you do | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Map buying process | Walk through the buyer's approval chain with your champion | A list of buyer-side steps with owners |
| 2. Co-create milestones | Populate the shared doc live on a call | Dated milestones committed to by the buyer |
| 3. Assign owners | Name a specific individual for each step | Accountability, not a wishlist |
| 4. Define success criteria | Specify what "done" looks like per milestone | Verifiable exit conditions, no drift |

> **Pro tip:** Build the MAP in a shared document the buyer can edit, not a PDF you email. A document the buyer can edit is a plan they own; a PDF is a sales asset they will ignore.

## Common Mutual Action Plan Mistakes That Stall Deals

The most damaging mutual action plan mistakes are seller-only authorship, omitting the buyer's internal approval steps, and letting dates slip without renegotiating them. Each of these turns the MAP from a shared accountability tool back into a seller agenda, which is the exact problem the MAP is meant to solve.

These three mistakes account for the majority of MAP failures we see in practice. The pattern is the same every time: a sales team adopts the format, skips the co-authorship step, and ends up with a document that looks collaborative but functions as a one-sided close plan.

### Mistake 1: Building the MAP Alone

The single most common mistake is a seller building the entire MAP in isolation and sending it to the buyer for "review." This defeats the purpose. The accountability in a MAP comes from the buyer co-authoring the milestones and committing to dates in real time. A MAP the buyer receives fully formed is, functionally, a close plan with better branding.

The fix is structural: never send a completed MAP. Send a blank template and a calendar invite for a 30-minute build session. If the buyer will not commit to a 30-minute working session, the deal is not as far along as you think, and the MAP would not have helped anyway.

### Mistake 2: Ignoring Procurement and Security Review Steps

The second mistake is listing only the seller's milestones and the buyer's sign-off, while omitting the buyer's internal procurement and security review steps. This is a failure of Step 1 in the build process. If the MAP does not include the buyer's security questionnaire, vendor onboarding, and legal redline as dated milestones, those steps will run on their own timeline and blow your close date.

Procurement and security are the two functions most likely to introduce a multi-week delay the seller did not see coming. A good MAP makes those delays visible weeks before they hit. A [Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2024/01/the-b2b-sales-playbook) analysis of complex B2B deals found that the deals most likely to stall are those where the seller discovers the buyer's internal review process late in the cycle, after the proposal is already out.

### Mistake 3: Letting Dates Slip Silently

The third mistake is allowing milestone dates to slip without renegotiating them in a live conversation. When a milestone moves, the temptation is to quietly update the date in the shared doc and keep going. The problem is that a slipped date changes every downstream date, and if those changes are not discussed, the MAP stops reflecting reality and the buyer stops trusting it.

The fix is a rule: any date that moves gets re-confirmed in the next deal call, along with the new downstream dates it affects. This keeps the MAP honest and signals to the buyer that the document is a live operating tool, not a record of what was once hoped for.

> "The deals that stall are almost never lost to a competitor. They stall because the buyer's internal process ran on a timeline the seller never mapped. A MAP makes that timeline visible, but only if you update it the moment it changes." — Abe Dearmer, GrowthGear Consulting

## How to Track and Maintain a Mutual Action Plan

Maintain a mutual action plan by reviewing it at the start of every deal call, updating dates the moment a milestone slips, and re-confirming the next two milestones with the buyer each time so shared accountability never lapses. A MAP that is not reviewed weekly is a MAP that has already stopped working.

Tracking is where most MAP initiatives die. Teams build a strong MAP in the kickoff session, then let it sit unreviewed for three weeks while the deal quietly drifts. By the time anyone opens the document again, half the dates are wrong and the buyer has lost faith in it as a source of truth.

### Weekly MAP Review Cadence

Run a standing weekly review for every deal above a threshold that has an active MAP. The review is short, five to ten minutes per deal, and answers three questions:

- Which milestones completed since last week?
- Which dates moved, and why?
- What are the next two milestones, and who owns them on the buyer's side?

This cadence keeps the MAP alive. The first question celebrates progress; the second keeps the document honest; the third keeps the buyer's next steps top of mind so they never lapse into silence. Teams that run this cadence report noticeably less end-of-quarter surprise slippage.

### Using Your CRM to Track MAP Milestones

Log MAP milestones in your CRM so the plan is visible beyond the shared document. Each milestone becomes an activity or task on the opportunity, with the due date and owner recorded. This gives sales leadership a view of where every deal sits in its buying process, not just in the seller's process.

For teams using AI-assisted [pipeline tracking](/b2b-sales/b2b-sales-pipeline-guide), logging MAP milestones as structured CRM data lets you surface slippage early. Tools that apply [AI to business workflows](https://ai.growthgear.com.au/machine-learning/how-to-implement-ai-in-business-complete-guide) can flag deals where two or more milestone dates have slipped in sequence, which is the leading indicator of a stalled deal. A well-maintained MAP also feeds cleanly into a [high-converting sales funnel](https://marketing.growthgear.com.au/content-marketing/how-to-create-high-converting-sales-funnels-complete-guide) by making the back end of the cycle predictable.
