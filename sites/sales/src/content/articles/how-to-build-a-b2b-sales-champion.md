Most B2B deals don't die because a rep failed to pitch well. They die because the internal advocate carrying the deal forward had less influence than the rep assumed. **Salesforce State of Sales research** cited across the industry shows that 75% of B2B buyers now expect a rep-guided digital journey, but that guidance only works inside the buyer's walls if someone on the inside is actually willing to push.

That person is your champion. Building one deliberately, rather than hoping the friendliest contact turns into an advocate, is one of the highest-leverage skills in enterprise and mid-market sales.

## What Is a Champion in B2B Sales?

A B2B sales champion is a person inside the buyer's organization who has influence and actively sells your solution internally because they have a personal stake in it being selected. This person acts as your advocate inside the buying committee, working the internal politics your sales calls can't reach. Without one, even a strong external pitch usually stalls against internal inertia.

> "A person with influence in the buying organization. They have an investment in your solution being selected." — [Force Management](https://www.forcemanagement.com/blog/meddic-vs.-meddpic-the-meaning-difference-and-benefits-of-each-for-sales-qualification-force-management), MEDDIC/MEDDPICC sales qualification framework

That definition sits inside the broader [MEDDIC sales methodology](/sales-techniques/meddic-sales-methodology-guide), where the champion is one of six qualification criteria reps check before trusting a deal's forecast. Skipping the champion check is one of the most common reasons deals that looked "qualified" on paper stall out mid-cycle.

Identifying a champion answers *whether* a deal is winnable. What a rep actually says to that champion is a separate problem, one Force Management's other flagship framework, [Command of the Message](/sales-techniques/command-of-the-message-methodology-guide), was built to solve — giving reps language the champion can repeat internally without a rep in the room.

### Champion vs. Coach vs. Economic Buyer

Confusing these three roles is a common, costly error. All three may seem cooperative on calls, but their leverage and willingness to advocate publicly differ sharply.

| Role | What They Do | How to Spot Them |
|---|---|---|
| **Champion** | Sells your solution internally to peers and leadership; has skin in the game on the outcome | Takes personal risk, advocates publicly, pushes for budget approval without being asked |
| **Coach** | Shares information, guidance, and internal intelligence about how the deal really moves | Gives you useful insight privately but avoids public advocacy or internal debate |
| **Economic Buyer** | Controls budget and holds final sign-off authority for the purchase | Focuses on ROI and risk, rarely engages in early technical or product discussion |

Knowing which role you're actually talking to changes how you run the deal. A rep who treats a coach like a champion wastes weeks waiting for advocacy that was never coming, and a rep who treats an economic buyer like a champion overloads a busy executive with detail they don't need this early in a [B2B sales cycle](/b2b-sales/b2b-sales-cycle-guide).

### Why a Single Champion Isn't Enough

Relying on one internal advocate leaves the deal exposed to internal conflict a single person can't resolve alone. Gartner's 2025 sales survey found that 74% of B2B buyer teams demonstrate unhealthy conflict during the buying decision process, driven by conflicting departmental objectives, disagreement on the best course of action, or an outside decision-maker overruling the group.

The same Gartner research found buying groups that reach consensus are 2.5 times more likely to report their deal was high-quality. A champion can advocate, but they usually cannot single-handedly force consensus across finance, IT, and the line-of-business owner. That's why champion-building has to pair with a broader multi-threading strategy rather than replace it — your champion becomes the person who helps you navigate to the other stakeholders, not a substitute for reaching them. A deal with one enthusiastic advocate and no other engaged contact is still, structurally, a single-threaded deal wearing a champion's name tag.

## How to Identify a Real Champion Early in the Deal

Identifying a true champion means distinguishing polite engagement from active advocacy. A real champion will sell internally on your behalf; a friendly contact just returns your emails and nods on calls. Confusing the two costs reps weeks of cultivating a relationship that was never going to convert into internal pressure.

### The Signals That Separate a Champion from a Friendly Contact

Look for observable behavior, not stated enthusiasm. Champions demonstrate willingness to spend political capital through action, not just tone on a call.

- **Proactive introductions**: They voluntarily introduce you to other stakeholders, like a technical evaluator or budget owner, without being prompted or coached.
- **Unprompted material requests**: They ask for specific materials, like a case study or ROI breakdown, to share internally before you've offered it.
- **Constructive pushback**: They push back on your pricing or scope not to stall the deal, but to help you fit it inside real internal constraints.
- **Personal stake**: They have something on the line — a KPI they own, a promotion tied to solving the problem, or a mandate from their own leadership.

### Questions to Ask in Discovery That Reveal Champion Potential

Targeted [discovery call questions](/sales-techniques/discovery-call-questions-that-uncover-buying-intent) surface whether a contact has both the willingness and the standing to advocate for you, well before you've built out a full proposal.

- "Who else needs to be involved before this becomes real for your team?"
- "What happens on your end if this doesn't get solved this quarter?"
- "Have you pushed something like this through before, and what was the hardest part?"
- "If we move forward, who's the first person you'd need to convince internally?"
- "How is success measured on this, and who holds you accountable for it?"

Answers that name specific people, specific processes, and specific personal consequences point to a real champion. Vague answers — "I'd have to check" or "probably a few people" — point to a coach at best.

Timing matters as much as the answer itself. A contact who names names and processes in the first or second call is showing you they've already thought through the internal path, which is a stronger signal than the same answer delivered only after weeks of relationship-building. If a contact still can't name a single other stakeholder after several conversations, that's informative on its own — either the deal genuinely has no other stakeholders (rare above a small deal size) or your contact doesn't have visibility into how the decision actually gets made.

## How to Test Whether Your Champion Can Actually Sell Internally

A champion's title or enthusiasm doesn't prove they can move the deal. Verbal support costs a contact nothing, so reps have to test actual influence before betting a deal's forecast on one person's word. The test should produce a concrete, observable result, not another conversation.

### The "Can They Get a Meeting" Test

The clearest test of a champion's influence is assigning a specific, time-bound task that requires them to spend real internal capital. Ask your champion to set up a meeting with a named stakeholder — the economic buyer, a technical evaluator, a department head — by a specific date.

Whether they deliver that meeting on schedule tells you more than anything they've said. A champion who secures the meeting has demonstrated they have both the access and the standing to move your deal. A champion who stalls, reschedules repeatedly, or comes back with "they're too busy right now" is signaling limited influence, even if their enthusiasm for your product hasn't wavered.

Run this test early, ideally before you've invested significant time building a proposal around this one relationship. A rep who waits until late-stage negotiation to discover their champion can't actually reach the economic buyer has already lost weeks that could have gone into building a second relationship in parallel. Treat the test as diagnostic, not adversarial — frame it to the champion as "let's get the right people in the room" rather than a test of their credibility, and most genuine champions will treat it the same way you do: as the next logical step in a deal they're already invested in.

### Warning Signs of a Powerless Champion

Watch for patterns that suggest you're working with a well-meaning coach rather than a real advocate:

- **Vague next steps**: They keep saying "I'll ask around" instead of naming actual people or committing to a concrete action.
- **No track record**: They've never successfully pushed a purchase or internal initiative through before.
- **Avoids leadership introductions**: They resist connecting you with their manager or other senior stakeholders.
- **Can't explain the process**: They don't know how budget actually gets approved at their company, which means they're disconnected from the real decision-making machinery.

If two or more of these show up, treat the relationship as a coach relationship rather than a champion relationship, and start mapping the rest of the buying committee instead of continuing to invest your limited time in them as your primary advocate. Reassigning the "champion" label doesn't mean cutting the contact off — a good coach is still valuable for internal intelligence — it just means your deal plan can no longer assume they'll carry the internal selling for you.

## How to Arm Your Champion With What They Actually Need

Champions don't need more product collateral — they need a short, forwardable business case they can defend in a room without you present. If they can't articulate the value clearly under questioning from a skeptical colleague, their advocacy collapses the moment you're not in the room.

### Build the Internal Business Case With Them, Not for Them

Co-building the case with your champion, instead of handing them a finished deck, ensures it actually reflects the specific political and operational realities of their organization. Ask them who else needs convincing and why, then build a one-page summary together that answers those specific internal objections. This is the same collaborative discipline behind a good [mutual action plan](/sales-techniques/mutual-action-plan-guide) — co-authoring in the room, not filling in blanks after the call and hoping they stick.

A champion who helped build the argument understands its nuances and can defend it under pushback. A champion handed a generic deck usually just forwards it as an attachment, unread, and hopes for the best.

### Give Them a Short, Forwardable Story, Not a Generic Deck

Generic product decks rarely persuade internal stakeholders who are focused on their own metrics, not your feature list. A 2026 study by Corporate Visions and the Florida State University Sales Institute, led by Dr. Leff Bonney, analyzed more than 4,000 real B2B sales pursuits and found that sellers who lead with product-focused pitches win only 18% of their deals — the lowest win rate of the four strategies the study tested. High-performing sellers used a perspective- or vision-led approach in 48% of their deals, compared with just 18% for underperforming sellers.

> "Sellers tend to lose when they run the same sales approach regardless of what the buyer needs within the context of the deal." — Dr. Leff Bonney, Director, Florida State University Sales Institute

Translate that into what you hand your champion: not a slide deck about your product, but a one-page story connecting the internal stakeholder's own problem to a specific, credible outcome. This is the same discipline behind strong [B2B content marketing](https://marketing.growthgear.com.au/content-marketing/best-content-marketing-strategies-b2b-companies) — lead with the reader's problem, not your feature set — applied to a single internal audience of one or two people instead of a broad campaign.

Champions also benefit from understanding where they sit in the buyer's own internal approval path, which mirrors the logic of a [sales funnel](https://marketing.growthgear.com.au/content-marketing/how-to-create-high-converting-sales-funnels-complete-guide) turned inward: awareness of the problem, internal consensus-building, then a final approval stage your champion has to personally shepherd.

## Common Champion-Building Mistakes That Kill Deals

The most common champion mistakes come down to over-reliance on one person, misreading access as influence, and under-arming the champion you do have. Each is avoidable with a more disciplined process, and each shows up repeatedly in stalled or lost deals.

### Relying on One Champion Instead of Multi-Threading

A single champion is a single point of failure. If they change jobs, lose political standing, or simply go quiet during a reorg, a deal built entirely around them stalls immediately with no backup relationship to fall back on. Champion-building should always run alongside — never replace — [multi-threading additional stakeholders](/b2b-sales/how-to-multi-thread-b2b-sales-deals) across the buying committee.

Some sales teams now track CRM signals and job-change alerts to catch a champion going quiet earlier, an approach that overlaps with how [AI tools get implemented in day-to-day business processes](https://ai.growthgear.com.au/machine-learning/how-to-implement-ai-in-business-complete-guide) — using automated signals to flag risk a rep would otherwise only notice weeks later.

### Confusing Access With Influence

It's easy to mistake frequent communication for real influence. HubSpot's 2024 B2B Buyer Survey of 422 B2B professionals found that 54% of buyers said they need buy-in from just one or two individuals before making a purchase decision. That's good news — you rarely need to win over an entire committee.

It only pays off if you've correctly identified which one or two people actually matter. A contact who always takes your calls isn't automatically one of them. Some of the most responsive contacts in a deal have no real sway over the economic buyer at all; they're easy to reach precisely because they have little else demanding their time. Confirm influence with the meeting test above before assuming access equals leverage.
