A sales pipeline review is the single discipline that separates teams that hit quota from teams that hope to. It is a structured inspection of every active deal, confirming that each one has a next step, a credible close date, and a real buyer. Without it, pipelines fill with stale opportunities that inflate forecasts and mask prospecting gaps. According to Salesforce State of Sales research, high-performing teams are 1.5x more likely to inspect their pipeline weekly than underperformers, and that weekly cadence is the difference between catching a stalled deal in week two and discovering it in week twelve.

This guide covers the cadence, agenda, and exit criteria for running a pipeline review that actually cleans deals, plus how to handle stalled opportunities and keep your coverage ratio honest.

## What Is a Sales Pipeline Review and Why It Matters

A sales pipeline review is a recurring meeting where a sales manager and each rep walk through every active deal in the CRM, verify it meets the exit criteria for its current stage, confirm the next step and close date, and remove or downgrade opportunities that no longer qualify. The output is a clean pipeline, an accurate forecast, and a list of deals that need executive intervention. That clean output is also the input every [sales forecasting method](/b2b-sales/sales-forecasting-methods-guide) depends on; no formula fixes a forecast built on stale stage data.

It is not a status update or a coaching session, though coaching happens naturally when a manager sees how a rep is working a deal. The reason it matters is simple: pipelines decay. Deals slip, buyers go quiet, champions leave their companies, and budgets freeze. A deal that entered the pipeline in March as a strong opportunity can become dead weight by June if nobody inspects it. HubSpot sales research found that 40 percent of forecasted deals slip from one quarter to the next, and a meaningful share of those never close at all. Without a review, those deals sit in the pipeline masquerading as revenue.

### Pipeline Review vs Pipeline Audit

A pipeline review is a regular, recurring practice, typically weekly or biweekly, focused on keeping the pipeline healthy day to day. A pipeline audit is a deeper, less frequent exercise, usually quarterly, that examines stage definitions, conversion rates, and whether the pipeline structure itself is serving the team. If a review is like checking the oil, an audit is like a service inspection. You need both, but the review is the one that runs on a clock.

### What a Clean Pipeline Looks Like

A clean pipeline has three properties. Every deal has a next step dated within the next seven days. Every deal has a close date that the rep can defend with a buyer commitment, not a guess. Every deal meets the exit criteria for its stage, meaning a deal labeled negotiation has a written proposal in the buyer's hands, not just a verbal maybe. [Pipeline management](/b2b-sales/sales-pipeline-management-guide) fails when these three properties slip, and the review is what enforces them.

## How Often Should You Run a Sales Pipeline Review

The right cadence depends on your team size, deal velocity, and quota performance, but the baseline is weekly for individual reps and biweekly for the full team. Gartner sales practice research recommends weekly pipeline inspection for any team that missed quota by more than 10 percent in the prior quarter, because the faster you spot a stalled deal, the more runway you have to replace it.

For teams consistently hitting number, biweekly is sufficient. The cost of waiting is measurable: deals reviewed biweekly instead of weekly spend an average of 12 extra days in the pipeline before a stalled deal is flagged.

> **Pro tip:** Run the rep-level review on Monday so the week starts with deal priorities set, and the team-level forecast roll-up on Friday so leadership sees where the quarter stands before the weekend.

### Cadence by Team Size

| Team size | Rep review cadence | Team forecast call | Deep quarterly scrub |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 to 5 reps | Weekly, 30 min | Weekly, 30 min | End of each quarter |
| 6 to 20 reps | Weekly, 45 min | Biweekly, 60 min | End of each quarter |
| 21 to 50 reps | Biweekly, 45 min per pod | Biweekly, 60 min | End of each quarter |
| 50+ reps | Weekly per pod, 45 min | Monthly roll-up | Monthly spot checks plus quarterly scrub |

### Signals You Need to Review More Often

Three signals tell you the cadence is too slow. First, your forecast accuracy is below 80 percent, meaning your predicted close revenue misses actual close revenue by more than 20 percent. Second, your [pipeline coverage ratio](/b2b-sales/pipeline-coverage-ratio-guide) drops below 3x in the first three weeks of a quarter, leaving no time to prospect. Third, more than 15 percent of your deals have been in the same stage for over 30 days. Any one of these means you should move to weekly reviews immediately.

## How to Structure a Sales Pipeline Review Agenda

A pipeline review agenda should cover five items in a fixed order: top deals by value, slipped deals, deals past their close date, stage violations, and a coverage check. This order matters because it forces the team to confront the biggest revenue risks first, and it keeps the meeting under 45 minutes.

Require every rep to update their CRM before the meeting, not during it; a review where reps are typing next steps in real time is a data entry session, not a review. According to Salesforce, reps spend only 34 percent of their time on selling activities, and a meeting where they do admin work instead of deal work wastes the 34 percent that matters.

### The Five-Item Agenda

1. **Top deals by value (15 min):** Walk through the five highest-value deals per rep. For each, confirm the next step, the close date, and the buyer's commitment. Flag any deal over 50 percent of the rep's quarterly quota for executive attention.
2. **Slipped deals (10 min):** Review every deal whose close date moved out by more than seven days since the last review. Ask the rep to name the event that caused the slip and the new event that will move it forward.
3. **Deals past close date (10 min):** Any deal whose close date has passed and is still open gets a 14-day deadline or moves to closed-lost. No exceptions.
4. **Stage violations (5 min):** Scan for deals with no next step, no close date, or a stage that does not match the exit criteria. These are the leading cause of forecast inaccuracy.
5. **Coverage check (5 min):** Divide total pipeline value by remaining quota. If below 3x, flag a prospecting gap. If above 5x, flag a qualification problem.

### Who Attends and How to Keep It Honest

The manager and the rep whose pipeline is under review attend every meeting. For deal-specific escalations, invite a sales engineer, deal desk, or legal resource for the relevant deal only. Skip the bulk all-hands format; one-on-one reviews produce more honest deal-by-deal conversations because reps are less likely to inflate a deal in front of a single manager than in front of the whole team. [Pipeline metrics](/b2b-sales/sales-pipeline-metrics-kpis-guide) should be visible to the manager before the meeting starts, not pulled up live, so the conversation focuses on judgment, not data retrieval.

## Which Deals to Cut From Your Pipeline During Review

Cutting deals is the most uncomfortable part of a pipeline review and the most valuable. A pipeline that never shrinks is a pipeline nobody trusts. The deals to cut fall into four categories: deals with no movement for 30 days, deals past their close date with no new event, deals where the champion has left the company, and deals where the buyer has gone silent after a proposal.

Each of these has a clear disqualifier, and the review's job is to apply it consistently rather than hoping a stalled deal will resurrect itself.

### The 30-Day No-Movement Rule

Any deal that has had no CRM activity, no email, no call, and no calendar invite for 30 days gets a 14-day deadline. If nothing happens in those 14 days, the deal moves to closed-lost with a reason code. This rule is the single most effective pipeline-cleaning tool because it removes the hope-driven deals that linger for months. HubSpot research shows that 44 percent of salespeople give up after one follow-up, but the pipeline review is not about follow-up, it is about honest assessment: a deal with no buyer engagement for a month is not a deal your follow-up will save.

### Stage-Specific Exit Criteria

| Stage | Exit criteria to stay | Cut signal |
|---|---|---|
| Prospecting | Confirmed meeting on the calendar | No meeting after 3 outreach attempts |
| Qualification | Budget, authority, need, timeline confirmed | Buyer declines to share budget or timeline |
| Discovery | Pain point documented and shared with buyer | No documented pain after 2 meetings |
| Proposal | Written proposal sent, verbal feedback received | No feedback 14 days after sending |
| Negotiation | Redlines exchanged, commercial terms discussed | No redline response 21 days after sending |
| Closing | Contract in legal review with a date | No legal engagement 30 days after verbal yes |

### Why Reps Resist Cutting and How to Handle It

Reps resist cutting because a full pipeline feels safe and removing a deal feels like losing. The counterargument is the data: a [b2b sales pipeline](/b2b-sales/b2b-sales-pipeline-guide) bloated with stalled deals produces worse forecasts, not better ones, because the stalled deals inflate the coverage ratio and hide the real prospecting gap. Frame the cut as a reclassification, not a loss: a deal moved to closed-lost with a clean reason code can be re-opened if the buyer returns, and it stops polluting the forecast in the meantime.

## How to Fix Stalled Deals After the Pipeline Review

Not every stalled deal should be cut. Some need a specific intervention that the review surfaces: an executive sponsor, a revised proposal, a new champion, or a change in scope. The pipeline review's output should include a short list of deals flagged for action, each with a named owner and a deadline. This is where the review transitions from inspection to problem-solving.

According to Harvard Business Review, companies that respond to leads within an hour are 7x more likely to qualify them, and the same urgency principle applies to stalled deals: the faster you act on a stalled deal flagged in a review, the more likely you are to recover it.

### The Three Recovery Plays

1. **Executive sponsor outreach (for large deals):** If a deal over 50 percent of quarterly quota is stalled in negotiation, the sales leader contacts the equivalent executive on the buyer side within 48 hours. This unblocks deals stuck on the buyer's internal politics, not on commercial terms.
2. **Proposal refresh (for deals stalled post-proposal):** If a buyer went silent after a proposal, send a revised proposal with a narrower scope or a time-limited pricing option within 7 days. The goal is to create a new event that restarts the conversation, not to chase the old one.
3. **Champion rebuild (for deals where the champion left):** If the buyer's champion left the company, identify a new contact within 14 days and schedule a re-qualification call. According to LinkedIn Sales Solutions, 54 percent of B2B buyers say their buying committee has changed in the past year, so champion turnover is a routine problem, not a death sentence.

### Tracking Recovery Success

Measure recovery rate weekly: the percentage of deals flagged for action in the review that move forward within 14 days. A healthy recovery rate is above 60 percent. Below 40 percent, your recovery plays are not working or your deals were already lost when flagged. Track this alongside your [pipeline report](/crm-tools/sales-pipeline-report-guide) so you can see whether the review is producing real deal movement or just busywork.

> **Common mistake:** Do not reassign a stalled deal to a new rep without a re-qualification call. A fresh rep without buyer context repeats the same mistakes that stalled the deal the first time.

A second metric worth tracking is time-to-recovery: the median number of days between a deal being flagged in the review and it either advancing a stage or being closed-lost. Teams with a median time-to-recovery under 10 days tend to recover 70 percent or more of their flagged deals. Teams where the median stretches past 20 days typically recover fewer than 30 percent, because the buyer has already moved on to a competitor or deferred the purchase entirely. The review is where you measure this lag, not where you discover it after the fact.

The pipeline review often surfaces a pattern that is not a rep problem but a lead quality problem: deals consistently stalling at the same stage across multiple reps. When that happens, the fix is not more coaching, it is a conversation with marketing about lead fit and scoring. ### Sales Pipeline Review: At a Glance

| Element | Standard | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Cadence | Weekly per rep, 45 min | Monthly or ad hoc |
| Deal with no next step | Add one or cut within 7 days | Allowed to sit in pipeline |
| Deal past close date | 14-day deadline or closed-lost | Close date moved repeatedly |
| Coverage ratio | 3x to 4x of quota | Below 3x (prospecting gap) or above 5x (qualification gap) |
| Forecast accuracy | Within 10 percent of actual | Off by more than 20 percent |
| Recovery rate of flagged deals | Above 60 percent in 14 days | Below 40 percent |
